INTRODUCTORY 



It is a matter of common knowledge that the most complete 
and interesting Greek Archaeological Collection in the world, now 
assembled at the Hermitage in Petrograd, was found at the old 
Greek colony in Kertch. During one of the periodical invasions 
of the barbarian in this neighborhood it is evident that the in- 
habitants of Kertch assembled their treasures and hid them in one 
of the large Scythian tumuli in the neighborhood of the town. 
There the collection rested until about 1856, at which time it was 
discovered and taken to Petrograd. 

The Architological Collection on exhibition at the galleries 
of the American Art Association was assembled in those places in 
Southern Russia where, at the beginning of the fifteenth century 
B.C., the Greek Colonists located on the early trading routes on 
the shores of the Black Sea. The story of Iphigenia's transpor- 
tation to the Temple of Artemis in Taurida, Crimea, is told in 
the Iliad. 

The map of Ancient Greece shows how the colonies spread to 
the north and the east, where the great rivers offered to enter- 
prising tradesmen facilities for penetrating into wild and un- 
known lands. Such colonies were dotted along the shores of the 
Black Sea in Roumania, Bulgaria and Russia. Many of them 
developed into large and flourishing cities, well and richly built, 
and their ruins and archaeological treasures have only recently 
come to light. It was in one of these colonies, namely, Olbia (the 
Greek word for "happy"), that I personally carried on excava- 
tions and superintended the work of the local peasants who did 
the excavating, and collected the objects now on exhibition by 
the American Art Association. The bulk of this work was done 
during the war and I had the greatest difficulty in guarding the 
collection from the Bolshevists and bringing it out of Russia. 
Notwithstanding all precautions, a considerable portion of the 
collection was lost during frequent inspections of my lodgings in 
Odessa by the Bolsheviks. Also some of the gold objects were 
stolen when I embarked from Odessa to Constantinople with the 
collection. 



The majority of the objects on exhibition were found at 
Olbia, in the old Greek cemeteries of this colony, but other objects 
were found in the Greek colony at Chersonesus, near Sebastapol, 
in the Crimea, — some at Panticapea, now Kertch, at Beresan and 
other places. 

Terra-cotta, glass, gold and bronze objects form the bulk of 
the collection, as is natural, since these objects are made from 
materials which best resist the inroads of time. 

Olbia was situated at the junction of the two great rivers 
flowing into the Black Sea from the north — Hypanis (now Bug) 
and Borysthenes (now Dnieper). From old documents and tradi- 
tions, it would appear that at the place where Olbia was built 
there stood a still older Greek colony called Borysthenia, after 
the river Borysthenes. According to Euscbius, the Milesians 
established a colony in 650 B.C. and called it "Olbia." This 
statement is probablv correct, because there has been found at 
Olbia large quantities- of Milesian pottery, bearing all the charac- 
teristics of the Avork of the seventh century B.C. The Greek 
colony at Olbia, according to Herodotus, lived for several cen- 
turies in friendly relations with her neighbors to the north and 
east, the Scythians. The Ionian traders brought their manufac- 
tured goods to this place, and from the fifth century on the trad- 
ers from Athens were here purchasing slaves, hides and wheat 
from the Scythians. About the third century B.C., the colony 
of Olbia had difficulty with the Gothic hordes which began to 
surround them, and these difficulties continued until 50 B.C., 
when the town was laid waste by the Goths. During the reign of 
Septimus Severus, in the second century A.D., Olbia again be- 
came a flourishing colony, with the support of the Romans. 
Again, in about 24-8 A.D., it was destroyed by the Goths, but was 
rebuilt and continued to exist till an unknown date. Excavation^ 
which have been made have brought to light the old Greek Avails 
surrounding the city, interesting Hellenic houses, and also ceme- 
teries of various dates. The principal cult Avas that of Achilles- 
Pontarchus and also of Apollo-Prostates, the patron of the 
Strategii. From various inscriptions found, it is evident that 
other Greek Deities were worshipped at this place. 

The early coins of this colony were fish-shaped and were of 
various sizes, made of copper. There Avere also quadrangular 
coins Avith the design of a dolphin and heads of AA'heat. Up to the 
present but one silver coin of the same design has been found. 



Later large round copper coins, similar to the Roman Aes grave 
were found, and still later specimens of gold and silver coins of the 
/Eginetic standard. 

The prosperity of Olbia is testified to by the large amount 
of beautiful and expensive glass vases and other objects of luxury 
brought from all parts of the then known world, which have been 
found, showing that the trade of Olbia extended as far as Egypt, 
Rome, Phoenicia, Carthage and the Northern countries. 

Many of the objects in this collection are similar to the best 
in the British Museum, the New York Metropolitan Museum and 
the Louvre; for instance, the green glass pitcher No. 530 — multi- 
colored — cup No. 551, vase No. 552. 

The collection of Terra-cottas includes almost all phases of 
the development of this art from prehistoric times. The best 
specimens are the large vase No. 95, kylix No. 64, and the 
double-faced rhyton No. 76. Vases similar to these are on 
exhibition in the British and Metropolitan Museums. 

Some of the figurines (children's toys), representing animals, 
are most interesting. These objects prove that the Greek 
artists in the fourth century B.C., or earlier than that, had made 
the acquaintance of the polar bear (No. 166), and the Indian 
rhinoceros (No. ]67). 

There are bronze objects and implements going back to the 
Bronze Age — two bronze axes, Nos. 205, and ending witli the 
artistic pitcher No. 225, and a statuette of Aphrodite, No. 230. 

Silver is represented by some articles of adornment and toilet 
articles, as, for instance, the beautifully finished mirror, No. 774. 
A similar mirror is in the possession of the British Museum, also 
found at Olbia, but of inferior quality. 

The collection of bead-necklaces is one of the largest — over 
one hundred strings. 

The collection of gold objects is not especially rich in 
variety, but includes some of the rarest specimens known, as, for 
instance, the two necklaces Nos. 664 and 706. 

Of special interest are the two frescoes, Nos. 329 and 330, 
representing Diana and Dionysius. These frescoes have pre- 
served their pristine colors. 

The large collection of surgical instruments gives a good 
idea of the medical art antedating the Christian era. 

Joseph Chmielowski.

CONDITIONS OF SALE 



I. Rejection of bids: Any bid which is not commensurate 
with the value of the article offered or which is merely a nominal 
or fractional advance may be rejected by the auctioneer if in his 
judgment such bid would be likely to affect the sale injuriously. 

II. The buyer: The highest bidder shall be the buyer, and 
if any dispute arises between two or more bidders, the auctioneer 
shall either decide the same or put up for re-sale the lot so in 
dispute. 

III. Identification and part payment by buyer: The name 
of the buyer of each lot shall be given immediately on the sale 
thereof, and when so required, each buyer shall sign a card giving 
the lot number, amount for which sold, and his or her name and 
address. 

Payment at the actual time of the sale shall be made of all 
or such part of the purchase prices as may be required. 

If the two foregoing conditions are not complied with, the lot 
or lots so purchased may at the option of the auctioneer be put 
up again and re-sold. 

IV. Risk after purchase: Title passes upon the fall of the 
auctioneer's hammer and thereafter neither the consignor nor the 
Association is responsible for the loss or any damage to any 
article occasioned by theft, fire, breakage or any other cause. 

V. Delivery of purchases: Delivery of any purchases will 
be made only upon payment of the total amount due for all 
purchases at the sale. 

Deliveries will be made at the place of sale or at the storage 
warehouse to which purchases may have been removed. 

Deliveries at the American Art Galleries will be made only 
between the hours of 9 A. M. and 1 P. M. on sales' days and on 
other days — except holidays, when no deliveries will be made — 
between the hours of 9 A. M. and 5 P. M. 

Deliveries at places of sale other than the American Art 
Galleries will be made only during the forenoon following the day 
of sale unless bv special notice or arrangement to the contrary. 



Deliveries at the storage warehouse to which goods may have 
been sent will be made on any day other than holidays between 
the hours of 9 and 5. 

Deliveries of any purchases of small articles likely to be lost, 
or mislaid may be made at the discretion of the auctioneer during 
the session of the sale at which they were sold. 

VI. Storage in default of prompt payment and calling for 
goods: Articles not paid for in full and either not called for by 
the purchaser or delivered upon his or her order by noon of the 
day following that of the sale will be turned over by the Associa- 
tion to some carter to be carried to and stored in some warehouse 
until the time of the delivery therefrom to the purchaser, and the 
cost of such cartage and storage will be charged against the pur- 
chaser and the risk of loss or damage occasioned by such removal 
or storage will be upon the purchaser. 

NOTE : The Limited space of the Delivery Rooms 
of the Association makes the above requirements 
necessary, and it is not alone for the benefit of the 
Association, but also for that of its patrons, whose 
goods otherwise would have to be so crowded as to 
be subject to damage and loss. 

VII. Shipping: Shipping, boxing or wrapping of purchases 
is a business in which the Association is in no wise engaged, and 
will not be performed by the Association for purchasers. The 
Association will, however, afford to purchasers every facility for 
employing at current and reasonable rates carriers and packers ; 
doing so, however, without any assumption of responsibility on 
its parfr for the acts and charges of the parties engaged for such 
service. 

VIII: Guaranty: The Association exercises great care to 
catalogue every lot correctly and endeavors therein and also at 
the actual time of sale to point out any error, defect or imperfec- 
tion, but guaranty is not made either by the owner or the 
Association of the correctness of the description, genuineness, 
authenticity or condition of any lot and no sale will be set aside 
on account of any incorrectness, error of cataloguing or imper- 
fection not noted or pointed out. Every lot is sold "as is" and 
without recourse. 



Ever}' lot is on public exhibition one or more days prior to its 
sale, and the Association will give consideration to the opinion of 
any trustworthy expert to the effect that any lot has been incor- 
rectly catalogued and in its judgment may thereafter sell the lot 
as catalogued or make mention of the opinion of such expert, who 
thereby will become responsible for such damage as might result 
were his opinion without foundation. 

IX. Buying on order: Buying or bidding by the Associa- 
tion for responsible parties on orders transmitted to it by mail, 
telegraph or telephone will be faithfully attended to without 
charge or commission. Any purchases so made will be subject to 
the foregoing conditions of sale except that, in the event of ;i 
purchase of a lot of one or more books by or for a purchaser who 
has not through himself or his agent been present at the exhibi- 
tion or sale, the Association will permit such lot to be returned 
within ten days from the date of sale and the purchase money will 
be refunded if the lot in any manner differs from its catalogue 
description. 

Orders for execution b} r the Association should be written and 
given with such plainness as to leave no room for misunderstand- 
ing. Not only should the lot number be given, but also the title, 
and bids should be stated to be so much for the lot, and when the 
lot consists of one or more volumes of books or objects of art, the 
bid per volume or piece should also be stated. If the one trans- 
mitting the order is unknown to the Association, a deposit should 
be sent or reference submitted. Shipping directions should also 
be given. 

Priced Catalogues: Priced copies of the catalogue or any 
session thereof, will be furnished by the Association at charges 
commensurate with the duties involved in copying the necessary 
information from the records of the Association.
